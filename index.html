Africa

Craig did not name a species. The plant belongs to Zamiaceae, genus almost certainly Encephalartos — African cycads whose leaflets are often glaucous, the “blue” of several species.

That wax reads stronger in dry air and often washes greener in humid South Florida. The genus name is Greek enkephalos + artos, “bread in the head,” for pith once baked as bread; the whole genus is CITES Appendix I. This is a garden specimen, not a wild identification.

Plate II

Family
Zamiaceae
Native range
Africa only
How to say it
en-sef-uh-LAR-tos
Etymology
“Bread in the head” — starchy pith

Nigeria / Sudan → the Cape

The same African genus: Encephalartos lives only on that continent, from Nigeria and Sudan through the Cape, in some 65–68 species.

They are living gymnosperms — cone-bearing, not palms. Every species is endangered or worse in the wild from collecting, and the genus is CITES Appendix I. The photograph is labeled only to genus; this book does not invent a species for it.

Plate III

Family
Arecaceae
Native range
Hispaniola
How to say it
ZOM-bee-uh an-tih-LAR-um
Etymology
From the Haitian place-name that became the genus

Haiti + Dominican Republic

Zombia is a genus of one, endemic to Hispaniola on dry hills, slopes, and ridges, often on serpentine soils. The armored trunk is persistent, spiny leaf sheaths on a clustered fan palm.

It is a popular ornamental, drought- and salt-tolerant. Status depends on the list: IUCN called it not threatened in 2006; a later West Indian palm review treated it as Vulnerable; the Dominican Republic’s 2016 vascular-plant red list treated it as Critically Endangered in-country, amid habitat loss in Haiti.

Plate IV

Family
Arecaceae
Native range
S. Thailand, Peninsular Malaysia, Singapore, Sumatra, Borneo
How to say it
sir-TOSS-tuh-kiss REN-duh
Etymology
Named for sealing wax — the scarlet crownshaft, not a wax crop

West of Wallace’s Line

Also called lipstick palm and pinang rajah, this is the only Cyrtostachys west of Wallace’s Line, at home in lowland peat swamp, riverbank, and tidal freshwater.

It clusters, and it is slow. Singapore Botanic Gardens uses it in the logo; NParks lists the native Singapore population as Critically Endangered. This book does not invent a global IUCN rank.

Plate V

Family
Poaceae
Native range
Southern China and Vietnam
How to say it
bam-BEW-suh CHUNG-ee-eye
Also called
Tropical, white, or emperor’s blue bamboo

Southern China · Vietnam

A clumping (non-running) bamboo whose young culms wear a persistent white wax that reads blue. Culms commonly reach 8–12 m.

In subtropical gardens — Florida, Hawaii, California — it is grown as an ornamental screen.

Plate VI

Family
Zingiberaceae
Native range
Thailand to Peninsular Malaysia
How to say it
ZIN-jih-ber spek-TAB-ih-lay
Etymology
The stacked bracts look like a beehive

Thailand → Peninsular Malaysia

Native from Thailand to Peninsular Malaysia (Kew, Plants of the World Online). The inflorescence is a stack of incurved bracts; the true flowers poke from the pouches.

It is related to culinary ginger and grown here as an ornamental. IUCN lists it as Data Deficient — a data-poor rank, not a finding that the plant is safe.

Plate VII

Two species in one photograph, as Craig captioned them.

Montgomery Palm

Veitchia arecina Becc.

Family
Arecaceae
Native range
Vanuatu (Efate in the conservation note)
How to say it
VEET-chee-uh ah-reh-SEE-nuh
Etymology
Named for Col. R. H. Montgomery, a friend of David Fairchild

Vanuatu

Also treated under the synonym Veitchia montgomeryana. A tall, slender, solitary pinnate palm with a green crownshaft and red fruit; it does well in tropical Florida.

IUCN lists it as Endangered (as V. montgomeryana), citing habitat loss.
